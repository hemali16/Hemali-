## SECTION 1: ORIGIN & FOUNDATION

### The Garland and the Twelve Rupees

At 12 years old, Hemali went with her mother to visit her mother's friend, who asked her to make a garland out of semi-precious stones. She made a few, and was paid 12 Indian Rupees — 75 paise per garland.

That payment became the first marker of her own worth. In her words: *"that is the importance what I got, I got the value of myself."* From that moment, a dream took root — she wanted to work, and she wanted to stand on her own feet.

A few years later, she told her father she wanted to work. Her father, the family's breadwinner, held the belief that a woman of the house should not work — but her conviction was strong enough that he couldn't stop her. Instead, he tested her. He called her to his office, then walked her into another office where a secretary was working. The secretary greeted him fluently in English — *"Hello, Mister Shah"* — and Hemali, coming from a vernacular medium, was struck by how confidently the woman spoke. Her father replied, then told the secretary, *"she will come with me,"* or "she's going with her boss" — and repeated this exercise across three different offices.

The lesson she absorbed: *"when woman works, people will look at them with different angles, that they are available."* It planted something in her young mind — a decision that she would never work *under* someone.

Her father then let her sit in his own office, with one instruction: don't tell anyone you're my daughter. People who came in would speak to her dismissively — until she revealed, *"No, I'm Mr. Shah's daughter,"* and their tone instantly changed. That contrast taught her something she still carries: *"who you are make lot of difference. The respect comes from who you are."* It was here, she says, that the seed of entrepreneurship was planted — an enthusiasm that, in her words, *"never allowed me to sleep."*

She married at 19. Soon after, she overheard her mother-in-law telling a friend: *"we will give what we can give, if they want something more, let them earn and let them do whatever they want to do."* That sentence became permission. *"That gave me the freedom that gave me the freedom that now I can work,"* she says — and she never looked back.

Of the two incidents, she sums it up simply: the first taught her entrepreneurship and the value of standing on her own; the second gave her the freedom to be independent.

### The Person Who Shaped Her

Two people anchor how Hemali operates today.

The first is her mother-in-law — strict, dominating, and the one who, paradoxically, gave her room to grow. Hemali moved into her in-laws' house at 17, cooking and running the household, yet her mother-in-law allowed her to complete her graduation and supported her through it. It was her mother-in-law's own remark — that if Hemali wanted more, she should earn it herself — that, without anyone intending it, pushed Hemali to become the first woman in her family, on either side, to go out and work.

What stayed with her most was her mother-in-law's courage: *"if the whole world is telling to do this, she would had the courage to do what she wants and she had the courage to say no."* Despite a physical condition that made sitting difficult, she refused to simply comply — *"I am not going to sit down because I am suffering"* — in an era when that kind of defiance was rare. Hemali still calls her a mentor today.

The second is her husband — fearless in a way she once was not. Hemali describes her younger self as full of phobias, frightened even of a mosquito or a cockroach. Watching her husband and mother-in-law's fearlessness changed her. Now, when she's stuck, she asks herself: *"what my husband would do"* — a man who, despite being a successful entrepreneur with his own doubts, *"sleeps over that and he comes out next day with clear mind."* From these two people, she says she has learned both her morality and her personal values.

### The Value Running Her Decisions

The throughline across both relationships is the same: *"nothing is impossible in this world."* It shows up as a refusal to wait until she feels ready — to act first, and let the confidence follow. As she put it directly: *"I believe most people wait until they feel ready, but if I believe that I want to do something, I'll make sure that I'll do it."*

She has lived this conviction literally — overcoming a phobia of water by learning to swim in the cold of early winter on purpose, so the fear of drowning among other swimmers wouldn't stop her; being labeled "technologically challenged" and responding by launching her first BNI chapter on Zoom, then later signing up for an AI intensive with Daniel Paul. The pattern repeats everywhere: she does the hard thing before she feels ready for it.

## SECTION 2: CHARACTER UNDER PRESSURE

### The Hardest Chapter

Hemali spent over 30 years building expertise as a beautician, growing into cosmetology, eventually working in a medical center where she wrote articles, built a product line, and learned laser treatments. After a visa renewal, she was told the business would absorb her into another role "when it happens" — and suddenly she was without work.

Rather than sit still, she turned toward a long-held passion: travel. That, combined with a visa issue her son was facing, became the seed of City One Tourism and Travel — a company she ran for 13 years before handing it to a general manager. Eventually the work became monotonous, her target market unclear, and her husband wanted her to focus more on family. A vacuum opened again.

She admits to a period of real frustration — even blaming her husband for the fact that, after so many years, she'd stepped away from City One. But she found her own reframe: *"when you have nothing to do, you can be more creative,"* and *"there is no age — I have changed my career and my life so many times in my life that nothing is impossible."*

During this same period, her knee gave out. A doctor recommended an operation; she refused, choosing yoga instead, and recovered — becoming, in her words, more fit than before. Layered on top of this was menopause — hormonal changes, hot flashes — lived across three continents (India, Uganda, and the US). Out of that convergence of yoga, fearlessness, geography, and her stated purpose — *"to impact millions of lives"* — She Travels.Life was born.

### The Risk That Defined Her

Hemali was approached to launch a BNI chapter, alongside a co-launch partner who was, in her words, more experienced and "more smarter." Her own son had been the one expected to take this on, and didn't offer her support. She said yes anyway, despite real fear: *"inside honestly I was really scared, I was not sure, so I was with fear and I was questioning my capability."*

She sought out a mentor and was told: *"Hemali, you be yourself, people are not buying chapter, people are buying you."* She was also told to "be the mother of the chapter." She leaned entirely on being genuine rather than experienced. The chapter launched in 2021; she has now completed five years running it.

She later tried to replicate that success with a second chapter, aiming for Hall of Fame status (growing membership to 35) — something she didn't achieve with the first chapter, but did with the second. That second chapter is where she locates her real, validated success.

## SECTION 3: THE WORK & THE WHY

### When She Knew

The clearest "this is it" moment threads through her response about success: launching that first BNI chapter despite fear, being told to simply be herself, and watching that authenticity — not expertise — become the thing that worked. She describes the outcome as a "truly great outcome," and the chapter she built reached its five-year mark. That validation repeated and deepened with her second chapter, where she achieved Hall of Fame recognition she hadn't reached the first time.

### What She Does Differently

Hemali draws a clear line between transactional selling and value-based service. Coaching one of her own staff on handling a client inquiry, she told her: *"the sale is not about doing the transaction, it's about creating the value for others — the client would like to come to you again and again."* This same value-first instinct runs through her vision for She Travels.Life — not selling trips, but creating transformation: *"she helped people collect not just memories, but a better version of themselves."*

### The Reframe That Changes Everything

Her clearest reframe is about solo and unfamiliar travel as a tool for self-discovery rather than a risk to be avoided. She describes guiding a fellow entrepreneur, Sangeeta, through Singapore by refusing the easy option (a cab) and instead navigating by MRT and Google Maps — deliberately choosing the unfamiliar. When Sangeeta later found herself without GPS on a Dubai road, she was able to ask a stranger for directions — something she couldn't have imagined doing before. Hemali's own solo trip to Madrid produced the same shift: fear going in, and on the way out, feeling *"so liberated... so proud of myself."* Her conclusion: *"traveling solo is the best thing what anybody can do... I feel more spiritual, more understanding, freedom, liberated."*

### The Work That Made Her Proud

The same BNI chapter story stands as her clearest proof point — fear and self-doubt transformed into five years of running something she built from nothing but her own authenticity, later repeated and validated again with a second, even more successful chapter.
